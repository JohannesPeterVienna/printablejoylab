Habit tracker

Type your habits, pick the month and fill in a circle every day you keep your promise to yourself.

Why tracking works

A habit tracker makes progress visible. Every filled circle is a small reward, and an unbroken row – a streak – becomes something you do not want to break. Ticking also forces a short daily check: did I do it today? On paper that check happens where you can see it, on the fridge or in your planner, instead of behind an app icon. This template prints your own habit names down the left side, one circle for every day of the month, the weekday initial above each date, and a “Done” box at the end of every row for the monthly count.

How to set it up

  1. Type your habits into the box, one per line – up to 15. Names longer than about 32 characters are shortened, so keep them crisp.
  2. Add a few empty rows (up to 10) for habits you may start mid-month. The page holds 24 rows at most.
  3. Choose the month, or All 12 months to get a 12-page PDF with your habits printed on every page.
  4. Pick the paper size, landscape or portrait, an accent color and the title style.

Landscape gives the 28–31 day columns more width and is the better choice for most people; portrait suits a narrow spot on a door or a binder.

Write habits you can tick

A row only works if the answer each evening is a clear yes or no. “Read more” never gets a tick; “Read 10 pages” does. Add the trigger in brackets if it helps – “Stretch (after coffee)”. Our article habit tracker ideas that actually stick has a table of vague goals rewritten as trackable rows, and explains why research does not support the popular “21 days” rule: in the best-known study, forming a habit took a median of 66 days.

A filled example: January with five habits

A tracker for January 2027 lists: Read 10 pages, Walk 20 minutes, Phone in kitchen by 10 pm, Spanish lesson, Floss – plus three empty rows. Each evening the owner fills in the circles while setting the alarm. By the end of the month, the “Read” row has 25 filled circles out of 31, “Walk” has 17, and the gaps in the walking row cluster on Saturdays and Sundays. The “Done” boxes get the numbers, and a note at the bottom of the page records the decision for February: arrange the weekend walks with a friend, keep the rest.

Tips for a tracker that sticks

  • Start small: three to five habits are plenty. Too many rows turn the tracker into a guilt list.
  • Tick at a fixed time: filling in a week from memory makes the page unreliable.
  • Never miss twice: one missed day is normal; two in a row is the start of a new pattern.
  • Review monthly: use the “Done” column to decide what to keep, change or drop.

Row height and circle size

Each habit row is up to 10 mm tall; with many rows the generator shrinks them so that everything fits on one page. The circles scale with the space for each day:

Page (31-day month)Circle diameterRow height with 24 rows
A4 landscapeabout 3.9 mm6.4 mm
US Letter landscapeabout 3.5 mm6.6 mm
A4 portraitabout 2.3 mm10 mm
A5 or Half Letter landscapeabout 2.4 mm3.7–4.0 mm

Shorter months get slightly larger circles; February on A4 landscape has circles of about 4.3 mm. The numbers explain the advice to use landscape: in portrait the circles are barely larger than a pen tip. On A5 and Half Letter, keep the list to about ten rows, so the rows stay tall enough to read the habit names.

Ways to fill the circles

  • Full or half: fill the circle when you did the habit completely and only half of it for a reduced version – ten minutes of reading instead of thirty still counts as showing up.
  • Dot for a planned rest day: a small dot in the middle marks a day you deliberately skipped, so the gap does not look like a failure when you review the month.
  • Two colors: one color for weekdays and another for weekends makes patterns easier to see.

Other uses for the grid

The layout is simply names down the side and one circle per day, so it works for more than personal habits. As a chore chart, each row is one child and one chore, such as “Mia – set the table”. In a classroom, each row is a student and a circle is filled when the reading log comes back. For plant care, each row is a plant and a filled circle means watered. The “Done” box then gives the monthly count for each row.

Paper and printing

A tracker hangs in one place for a whole month and gets touched every day. 120 g/m² paper stays flat on a fridge or door and does not crease when you pin it up. The circles are outlined in a light gray, so they print clearly in grayscale and on any home printer.

Printing tips

Print the whole year in one go and keep the sheets in a folder next to the current page, so a new month is always ready. The circles are drawn in a light gray outline, so any pen color shows; a highlighter fills them quickly. For a yearly view, color one habit day by day on a yearly calendar.

Questions

How many habits can I track?

Up to 15 named habits plus empty rows, 24 rows at most – but most people do best with three to seven.

Can I track habits weekly instead of daily?

Use the checklist template with numbered lines for weekly goals, or mark only certain days on the monthly tracker.

Why does the tracker show weekday letters?

They help you spot patterns, such as habits that are always missed on weekends or on one particular weekday.

Can I track numbers, like hours of sleep?

Yes – write the number inside the circle instead of filling it in. Keep such rows to one or two, as they take longer to fill in.

How big are the circles?

About 3.9 mm on A4 landscape and 3.5 mm on US Letter landscape in a 31-day month. In portrait they shrink to about 2.3 mm, which is why landscape is the better choice for most people.

Can I use it as a chore chart for several children?

Yes. Type one line per child and chore, such as “Ben – feed the cat”. Each row gets its own circles and its own “Done” count for the month.

Does the tracker follow a Monday or Sunday week?

Neither: the columns are simply the days of the month from 1 to 28–31. The weekday letter above each date shows where the weekends fall.
