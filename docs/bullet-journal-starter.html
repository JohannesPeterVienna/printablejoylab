Bullet journal for beginners: a starter kit you can print

Rapid logging, the core collections and a first month set up step by step.

Reviewed November 3, 2026 · Printable Joy Lab editorial team

Bullet journaling is a paper planning method developed by designer Ryder Carroll and described in his book The Bullet Journal Method (2018). It needs no artistic skill and no special notebook: short notes, a handful of symbols and a few pages that you set up yourself. This guide explains the core ideas, sets up a first month page by page, and shows where printed pages are a good alternative to a bound notebook.

Rapid logging: short entries with symbols

Instead of full sentences you write short entries, each starting with a symbol that says what kind of entry it is. The symbol changes as the entry's status changes, so a glance at the page shows what is still open.

SymbolMeaningExample
•Task, still open• Book car service
×Task done× Pay electricity bill
>Task migrated (moved to a later page)> Sort tax papers
<Task scheduled into the future log< Renew passport (June)
○Event○ Anna's birthday
–Note– Plumber recommends new valve

Many people add a few signifiers in front of the symbol, such as an asterisk for priority. Start with the six symbols above and add your own only when you miss something.

The core collections

A “collection” is any page or group of pages with one topic. The method starts with four of them:

  1. Index – the first two pages. Number every page as you go and note topics with page numbers, for example “March 2027: 9–12”.
  2. Future log – events and tasks for the coming months. A printed yearly calendar makes a quick overview page next to it.
  3. Monthly log – a list of the month's dates on the left page, tasks for the month on the right. A printed monthly calendar is a handy alternative if you prefer a grid.
  4. Daily log – rapid logging, day by day, written as the day happens.

Many users add a weekly log between monthly and daily pages. It is not part of the original core, but it is one of the most popular additions; our weekly planner prints one ready-made.

Migration: the monthly clean-up

At the end of each month – and, if you like, at the end of each week – you look through open tasks. Each one gets one of three decisions: do it now, move it to the new monthly log (mark it with >) or schedule it into the future log (mark it with <). If a task is not worth rewriting, cross it out – it probably was not worth doing. This deliberate rewriting is what keeps the journal from turning into an endless list. Plan about 15 minutes for it; with 20 or more open tasks it is a sign that the monthly list was too ambitious, and the next one should be shorter.

Worked example: setting up January 2027

Here is a complete setup for a new journal on 5 mm dot grid, page by page. It takes about 30 minutes.

  • Pages 1–2: Index. Write “Index” at the top of both pages and leave them empty for now.
  • Pages 3–4: Future log. Divide each page into three boxes – six months across two pages. On an A5 dot grid page (27 × 38 dots in our generator) each box is about 12 dots high. Enter known dates such as birthdays and trips.
  • Page 5: Monthly log, calendar side. Write the numbers 1–31 down the left edge, one per row of dots, with the weekday initial next to each. January 1, 2027 is a Friday.
  • Page 6: Monthly log, task side. List what should happen in January: “• Book dentist”, “• Plan spring trip”.
  • Page 7: Habit collection. A small grid with 31 columns and three or four rows, or a printed habit tracker glued or clipped in.
  • Page 8 onward: Daily log. Write today's date as a heading and start logging.
  • Back to the index: add “Future log 3–4”, “January 2027 5–8”.

On January 31, migrate open tasks to a new February monthly log and add “February 2027” to the index.

A good middle path: print 5 mm dot grid for the pages you design yourself and the ready-made templates for calendars and trackers, then keep them together in an A5 binder. A5 is exactly half of A4, so you can also fold A4 sheets.

Keep it simple

Social media shows elaborate spreads with lettering and illustrations. They are a hobby of their own, not a requirement. The method works with plain handwriting, a ruler and a pen that does not bleed through. Spend the time on planning unless decorating is what you enjoy.

FAQ

Do I need dot grid paper?

No, any paper works. Dot grid is popular because the dots help with boxes and columns without getting in the way of handwriting.

What if I miss a few days?

Nothing breaks. Write today's date and continue; the index and monthly log keep everything findable.

How is a bullet journal different from a planner?

A planner comes with fixed pages for every day and week, whether you need them or not. A bullet journal grows page by page: a busy day can take two pages, a quiet week a few lines, and new collections appear only when you need them.

Which pen should I use?

Any pen that does not bleed through your paper. Test it on the back page first; gel pens and fineliners usually work on 90 g/m² and heavier, while alcohol markers bleed through almost any office paper.

Should every month look the same?

Ideally yes for the first few months. Repeating a simple setup makes it fast, and you will learn which parts you actually use.
