Meal planner

Plan the week's meals in one grid and write the shopping list right next to it – sorted by category so you shop faster.

Why plan meals?

Deciding what to eat every evening takes surprising energy, and the decision usually comes when everyone is already hungry. Planning the week in one go means one main shopping trip, less food thrown away and fewer last-minute takeaways. It also lets you use leftovers on purpose instead of rediscovering them at the back of the fridge. This template puts the whole week on a single sheet: a grid with seven day columns and one row per meal, and a grocery list sorted into five groups right next to it.

How to set it up

  1. Choose the meals: breakfast, lunch and dinner; all three plus snacks; lunch and dinner; or dinner only. Fewer rows give taller cells – on A4 landscape, three meals give cells of about 27 × 52 mm.
  2. Choose Monday or Sunday as the first day.
  3. Optionally enter a start date. Pick a date that falls on your first day of the week, so names and dates line up.
  4. Keep the grocery list on, or switch it off to give the meal grid the whole page – useful if you shop online and keep the list in an app.
  5. Landscape puts the list in a column on the right; portrait moves it to a strip along the bottom.

A quick weekly routine

Look at the calendar first and mark the busy evenings for quick meals or leftovers. Then check the fridge and plan the first days around what needs using up. Fill the dinner row, then breakfasts and lunches, and finally write everything you are missing into the grocery list. Our guide meal planning on one page breaks this into a timed 20-minute routine and includes the USDA guidance that cooked leftovers should be eaten within three to four days.

A filled example: dinners only

A couple who both work late plans dinners only, so each cell is tall enough for a recipe name and a page number. Monday: chili, double batch. Tuesday: chili with rice – arrow from Monday. Wednesday: stir-fry with the vegetables bought on Saturday. Thursday: “quick” – pasta with pesto. Friday: pizza night, homemade dough started Thursday. Saturday: eat out. Sunday: roast vegetables and fish. The grocery list reads: Produce peppers, onions, broccoli, carrots, basil; Protein ground beef, kidney beans, salmon; Dairy mozzarella, parmesan; Pantry rice, pasta, flour, canned tomatoes, yeast; Other baking paper.

Grocery list by category

The five groups – produce, protein, dairy, pantry and other – follow roughly the way many supermarkets are laid out. Writing each item into its group as you plan saves walking back and forth in the store, and an empty group tells you at a glance that you have, for example, planned no vegetables yet. If you shop at two stores, mark the items for the second store with a small letter in front.

Printing tips

A printed plan on the fridge answers the daily “What's for dinner?” question for the whole household; landscape works best there. Portrait suits binders, where you can keep past weeks as a library of meals that worked. If you track food spending, carry the receipt total over to the groceries line of the budget planner.

Questions

Can I start the week on Sunday?

Yes – switch “Week starts on” to Sunday. Enter a start date to have the dates printed above each day.

Is there a version without the grocery list?

Untick “Grocery list” and the meal grid uses the full page.

Can I plan two weeks on one page?

Not on one page, but you can print two copies and enter the start dates of consecutive weeks.

Where do recipes go?

Write the recipe name and where to find it – a cookbook page or a website name – in the cell. Dinner-only plans leave the most room.
