Meal planning on one page: a 20-minute weekly routine

Plan seven days of meals and a sorted grocery list on one sheet – with leftover timing that stays food-safe.

Reviewed November 3, 2026 · AdStern

Most meal plans fail for one of two reasons: they are too ambitious, or they live somewhere nobody looks. A single printed page on the fridge solves the second problem. A fixed routine of about twenty minutes solves the first, because it forces you to plan around the week you will really have, not an ideal one. This guide walks through that routine with our meal planner, shows a filled example and adds the food-safety numbers that matter for leftovers.

What goes on the page

The meal planner prints a grid with seven day columns and one row per meal, plus a grocery list. You choose the rows: breakfast, lunch and dinner; all three plus snacks; lunch and dinner; or dinner only. Fewer rows mean taller cells. On A4 landscape with three meals, each cell is about 27 × 52 mm; with dinner only, a cell is the full height of the grid. The grocery list is split into five groups – produce, protein, dairy, pantry and other – and sits in a column on the right in landscape or as a strip along the bottom in portrait.

If you enter a start date, the day headings show the dates. Enter a date that falls on the first day of your week (a Monday if the week starts on Monday, a Sunday if it starts on Sunday), so the weekday names and dates line up.

The 20-minute routine

StepMinutesResult on the page
1. Check the calendar3Busy evenings marked “quick” or “leftovers”
2. Check fridge, freezer and pantry4Ingredients that need using up placed in the first two days
3. Fill the dinner row5Seven dinners, including one or two planned leftovers
4. Fill breakfasts and lunches3Repeating simple options; lunches from dinner leftovers
5. Write the grocery list5Everything missing, written straight into the five groups

Do the routine at the same time each week, ideally the day before your main shopping trip, so the list goes straight into your bag. Start with dinners because they need the most cooking and drive most of the shopping. Breakfasts and lunches are often the same few options on repeat, and that is fine – variety is not the goal of the plan, fewer decisions are.

Three techniques that make the plan realistic

  • Theme nights: give some days a fixed theme such as pasta, soup, a vegetarian dish or “clean out the fridge”. Choosing a recipe inside a theme takes seconds.
  • Cook once, eat twice: double one or two dinners and write the second portion into a later lunch or dinner cell. Mark it with an arrow so everyone knows it is already covered.
  • A quick meal for the hardest day: one evening in most weeks is crowded by sports, overtime or appointments. Plan something that takes under 15 minutes or comes from the freezer, instead of pretending you will cook from scratch.

Leftovers: the food-safety numbers

Planned leftovers only help if they are eaten in time. The US Department of Agriculture's Food Safety and Inspection Service advises keeping the refrigerator at 40 °F (4 °C) or below, getting cooked food into the fridge within two hours, and eating refrigerated leftovers within three to four days – or freezing them. For the plan this means:

  • A dinner cooked on Sunday can be planned for Monday, Tuesday or Wednesday – not for Friday.
  • If a second portion would land later than that, write “freeze” next to it and move it to next week's plan.
  • Label containers with the cooking date; the planner's date headings make it easy to copy.

Worked example: a week for a household of three

This is an illustrative plan for two adults and a child, week starting Monday, with football practice on Tuesday and Thursday evenings. It uses the “breakfast, lunch, dinner” rows.

  • Monday: dinner – vegetable lasagna, double batch. The spinach and zucchini from last week are used up here.
  • Tuesday (practice): dinner – lasagna leftovers, 10 minutes to reheat. Lunch – bean salad.
  • Wednesday: soup night – lentil soup with bread; extra portion written into Thursday's lunch cell.
  • Thursday (practice): dinner – quesadillas, 15 minutes.
  • Friday: pasta night with tomato sauce; the remaining sauce is frozen.
  • Saturday: “clean out the fridge” – omelets with whatever vegetables are left.
  • Sunday: roast chicken with potatoes; the leftover chicken goes into Monday's lunch wraps.
  • Breakfasts: oatmeal on weekdays, pancakes on Saturday.

The grocery list for this plan, sorted into the printed groups, might read: Produce – onions, carrots, tomatoes, lettuce, potatoes, apples; Protein – whole chicken, eggs, lentils, canned beans; Dairy – mozzarella, milk, yogurt, cheddar; Pantry – lasagna sheets, pasta, oats, tortillas, flour; Other – foil, dish soap. Writing the list by group means one walk through the store instead of three.

Keeping it going

  • Keep old plans in a folder. After a month you have a personal list of meals that worked, and planning gets faster every week.
  • If you also track spending, the grocery total fits the groceries line in the budget planner.
  • For a fixed weekly shopping list that rarely changes, the checklist maker can print it pre-filled.
  • Plan meals as part of your weekly review, so busy days are visible when you choose the quick meals.

FAQ

Should I plan all three meals?

Only if you need to. Many households plan dinners only and keep breakfast and lunch on a short rotation; the “dinner only” option gives you large cells for recipe notes.

What if plans change mid-week?

Swap two days with an arrow. The groceries are already in the house, so the order matters less than the list.

How long can I keep cooked food in the fridge?

The USDA guidance is three to four days at 40 °F (4 °C) or below. Freeze anything you will not eat in that window.

Sources and standards

  • USDA Food Safety and Inspection Service – consumer guidance on leftovers and refrigerator temperatures (40 °F / 4 °C, two-hour rule, three to four days).
  • ISO 8601 – the date format YYYY-MM-DD, handy for labeling containers without US/European ambiguity.
  • Cell sizes are measured from the meal planner template (A4 landscape). Meals in the example are illustrations, not nutrition advice.
