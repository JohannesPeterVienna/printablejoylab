How to plan a week on paper (with the weekly planner)

A 25-minute weekly review, a capacity check in hours, and a filled example page.

Reviewed November 3, 2026 · Printable Joy Lab editorial team

A weekly plan is the sweet spot of paper planning. A day is too short to see trade-offs, a month too long to decide what happens next. On one sheet you can see all seven days, the tasks that need to happen and the space that is actually left. This guide describes a routine that takes about 25 minutes, a simple way to check whether your plan fits, and a filled example of our weekly planner.

Set up the page

Open the weekly planner and decide three things before you print.

  • Dated or undated. Enter the first day of the week and every box gets its weekday and date. The planner starts on whatever day you enter: enter a Monday for a Monday-to-Sunday week, or a Sunday if your week starts on Sunday. Leave the field empty to print undated pages that run Monday to Sunday.
  • Landscape or portrait. Landscape gives eight tall boxes in two rows of four and a 60 mm to-do column on the right; on A4 each day box is about 49 × 80 mm with ten writing lines. Portrait arranges the boxes in four rows of two and puts a 42 mm high to-do strip at the bottom, which suits ring binders.
  • Writing lines. The lines in the day boxes are 6.5 mm apart. Turn them off if you prefer to draw time blocks or small sketches.

The eighth box is labeled Notes. It is the place for things that belong to the week but not to a day: a phone number, a shopping item, an idea you do not want to lose.

The 25-minute weekly review

Do this at the same time every week, for example on Sunday afternoon or Friday after work. The times are guidelines; what matters is the order.

StepMinutesWhat you do
1. Look back5Read last week's page. Tick what is done, circle what moves forward, cross out what no longer matters.
2. Fixed appointments5Copy meetings, classes, school pick-ups and deadlines from your digital calendar or monthly page into the day boxes.
3. Collect tasks5Write every open task you know of into the to-do column. Do not sort yet.
4. Choose three priorities5Mark the three tasks that would make the week a success and give each one a day.
5. Capacity check5Estimate hours and compare with the free time you really have (see below). Move or drop tasks until it fits.

The look-back step is the one most people skip, and it is the one that keeps the system honest: a task that has moved forward three weeks in a row needs a decision, not another arrow.

The capacity check in hours

Paper planners fail quietly when a week holds twice as much as fits. A rough calculation prevents that:

  1. Write down your available working hours for the week, for example 40.
  2. Subtract the hours already taken by fixed appointments.
  3. Subtract a buffer for interruptions, email and small requests. A buffer of 20–30% of the remaining time is a sensible starting point; adjust it after a few weeks based on what really happened.
  4. Add up the estimated hours of your priorities and other planned tasks.

If the tasks need more time than is left, the plan does not fit. Decide now what moves to next week instead of finding out on Thursday evening. The same calculation works for a household week with “free evening hours” instead of working hours.

Worked example: one filled page

Here is how a page for the week of Monday, March 15, 2027 (ISO week 11) could look for someone working full-time with two evening commitments. The example is made up to illustrate the method.

  • Header: title “Weekly plan”, date range Mar 15 – Mar 21, 2027 printed automatically.
  • Monday: 9:00 team meeting (1 h), 14:00 dentist. Priority 1: draft the project report – block 10:30–12:30.
  • Tuesday: report, second block 9:00–11:00; 18:30 choir.
  • Wednesday: 13:00–15:00 client workshop. Priority 2: prepare workshop slides on Tuesday afternoon (written there as well).
  • Thursday: buffer morning, no new tasks. 19:00 parents' evening.
  • Friday: Priority 3: send the report; 30 minutes to plan next week.
  • Saturday and Sunday: shopping, a bike ride, nothing work-related.
  • To-do column: eleven smaller tasks such as “renew library card” or “reply to Sam about dates”, each with a check box.
  • Capacity check in the Notes box: 40 h available − 9 h of meetings = 31 h; minus a 25% buffer ≈ 23 h; priorities estimated at 12 h, smaller tasks at 8 h – total 20 h, so the plan fits.

Keeping the page alive during the week

  • Morning, two minutes: read today's box and the to-do column. Choose what happens next.
  • When something new comes in: write it in the to-do column, not straight into a day. It gets a day at the next review unless it is urgent.
  • When a task moves: draw an arrow and write it into the new day. Moving is fine; moving without deciding is the problem.
  • Busy days: add a daily planner page with an hourly schedule for the days that are fully booked.

If you track routines such as exercise or reading, a monthly habit tracker next to the weekly page works better than squeezing them into the day boxes. For the bigger picture, keep a monthly calendar in front of the weekly pages.

FAQ

Should I plan on Sunday or Friday?

Either works. Friday planning uses the knowledge you have at the end of the work week; Sunday planning feels fresher. Pick one and keep it fixed for a month before you judge.

What if my week starts on Sunday?

Enter a Sunday as the start date. The boxes then run Sunday to Saturday with the correct dates.

How many tasks should go on the to-do list?

As many as you know of, but only three are priorities. In landscape on A4 the column holds 22 lines, which is a useful natural limit.

Do I still need a digital calendar?

For reminders and shared appointments, usually yes. Our guide paper or app? describes a simple way to combine both.

Sources and standards

  • ISO 8601 – week numbering (weeks start on Monday; week 11 of 2027 runs from March 15 to March 21).
  • Box sizes and line counts are measured from the weekly planner template itself (A4, default settings). The buffer percentage is a planning suggestion, not a research figure.
